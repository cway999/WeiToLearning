Time Series Forecasting — Interview Question Bank

ImportantImportant notes from common mistakes
  • ADF vs KPSS: ADF commonly tests a unit-root null; KPSS tests a level- or trend-stationarity null. A failure to reject either null does not prove it.
  • Ljung–Box: There is no universal requirement that p > 0.10; interpret the selected lag, null, power, and residual plots.
  • Dynamic regression: Not all nonstationary variables must automatically be differenced; cointegration and valid long-run relationships matter.
  • ECM: The coefficient on the equilibrium error is not always negative; sign depends on the residual convention and which variable adjusts.
  • Weekly yearly seasonality: One calendar year contains approximately 52.18 weeks, not exactly 52.
  • Quarterly-to-monthly interpolation: Interpolation creates an assumed path, not newly observed monthly data.
  • Forecasting vs causation: Granger causality is predictive content, not identification of a structural intervention effect.
  • Forecast evaluation: Training residuals and forecasts evaluated with realized future predictor values are not substitutes for honest ex-ante backtests.

Q01. What makes time series data different from ordinary tabular data?

Difficulty: Easy · Based on source notes

NoteReference Answer

Time series observations have an order and often depend on earlier observations. Trends, seasonality, and changing data-generating processes mean that ordinary i.i.d. assumptions and random train/test splits can fail.

TipDetailed Explanation

A table of 1,000 independent customer profiles can often be randomly split into training and test sets. A table of 1,000 consecutive daily sales observations cannot: yesterday’s sales affect today’s sales, and December demand differs from July demand. If we randomly mix dates, the training set may contain information from the future relative to some test observations. That makes the reported accuracy overly optimistic.

Interview example: For next month’s home-price appreciation, you must specify the forecast origin (the date you issue the prediction), the target month, and which economic indicators were actually published at the origin. Time order is part of the problem definition, not just a sorting preference.

Q02. What is the difference between a time series forecasting model, an explanatory model, and a hybrid model?

Difficulty: Easy · Based on source notes

NoteReference Answer

A pure time series model predicts from the history of the target; an explanatory model uses predictors such as income or interest rates; a hybrid or dynamic regression model combines explanatory variables with time-dependent errors or lagged outcomes.

TipDetailed Explanation

To forecast retail sales, a seasonal ARIMA model may use only past sales. A regression model may include advertising, prices, holidays, and store openings. A hybrid model can include those predictors and ARIMA errors to capture temporal patterns left in the residuals.

The tradeoff is availability: future values of advertising expenditure can be planned, but future unemployment might itself need a forecast. Explanatory variables can improve both interpretation and accuracy, yet a model with theoretically meaningful covariates is not automatically causal or more accurate.

Q03. What are trend, seasonality, cycle, and irregular variation?

Difficulty: Easy · Based on source notes

NoteReference Answer

Trend is a long-run direction; seasonality repeats at a fairly regular calendar frequency; cycles fluctuate without a fixed period; irregular variation is what remains unexplained by those patterns.

TipDetailed Explanation

Monthly airline passengers might grow over several years (trend), peak every summer (seasonality), decline during an uncertain-length recession (cycle), and experience a one-day flight disruption (irregular shock). Trend need not be linear, and a cycle need not last exactly one year.

Confusing trend with seasonality leads to poor model choice. Repeated December spikes suggest seasonal structure, while a multi-year upswing and downturn might reflect business-cycle forces whose timing is not fixed.

Q04. What is a seasonal period, and how do you choose it for daily, weekly, or monthly data?

Difficulty: Easy · Based on source notes

NoteReference Answer

The seasonal period is the approximate number of observations per repeating cycle: 7 for weekly seasonality in daily data, 12 for annual seasonality in monthly data, and about 52.18 for annual seasonality in weekly data.

TipDetailed Explanation

For monthly data, a recurring December effect appears roughly every 12 observations. For daily data, both weekly seasonality (7) and annual calendar patterns can matter. Weekly data have about 365.25/7 = 52.18 weeks per year, not exactly 52; daily yearly patterns also do not fit a fixed 365-day period perfectly because of leap years.

Some models require an integer seasonal period; others use Fourier terms or calendar indicators for long or non-integer periodicities. Model the actual business calendar rather than mechanically setting every annual seasonal period to 52 or 365.

Q05. What is an autocorrelation function (ACF)?

Difficulty: Easy · Based on source notes

NoteReference Answer

The ACF at lag k measures the linear correlation between a series and its value k periods earlier, under a suitable stationary setting.

TipDetailed Explanation

If high sales tend to follow high sales, the lag-1 ACF is positive. If demand alternates up and down, some lags can have negative correlations. An ACF plot shows estimated correlations across lag lengths.

For monthly sales, large spikes around lags 12 and 24 can suggest yearly seasonality. A slowly declining ACF can suggest a persistent trend or unit root, but is not proof of one. Interpretation requires considering uncertainty bands, sample size, and the effects of preprocessing.

Q06. What is the difference between residuals and out-of-sample forecast errors?

Difficulty: Easy · Based on source notes

NoteReference Answer

A residual compares an observed value with the model’s in-sample fitted value; an out-of-sample forecast error compares a realized future outcome with a forecast made before that outcome was known.

TipDetailed Explanation

Suppose a model fitted using data through December predicts December sales as 105 when actual December sales were 110. That is a fitted residual of +5. If the same model, fitted only through November, predicted December sales as 101, the genuine one-step-ahead error would be +9.

Training residuals can be small even for an overfit model. Forecast accuracy must be measured with data unavailable at the time each prediction was issued. This is why time-series backtesting is central to model selection.

Q07. What is a naïve forecast, and why is it an important baseline?

Difficulty: Easy · Based on source notes

NoteReference Answer

A naïve forecast uses the latest observation as the next forecast; a seasonal naïve forecast uses the value from the same season in the previous cycle. Strong models should outperform sensible baselines.

TipDetailed Explanation

If this month’s home-price index is 250, a naïve forecast for next month is 250. If last December’s sales were 1,200 units, a seasonal naïve forecast for the coming December may be 1,200 units.

A sophisticated neural network that underperforms these baselines provides little business value. Baselines also identify whether the problem is intrinsically difficult or whether the model is failing to capture obvious persistence and seasonality.

Q08. What are white noise and independent identically distributed (i.i.d.) noise?

Difficulty: Easy · Based on source notes

NoteReference Answer

White noise has zero mean, constant finite variance, and zero autocovariance across distinct times; it need not be independent or normally distributed. I.i.d. noise additionally requires independence and identical distributions.

TipDetailed Explanation

A model can have residuals with no detectable linear autocorrelation but still have volatility clustering: very large errors tend to be followed by large errors. Squared residuals may be correlated even when residual levels are not. Such a sequence is not necessarily i.i.d.

For forecast evaluation, the aim is to leave no predictable conditional mean signal in the residuals. A white-noise diagnostic is useful, but it does not guarantee the prediction distribution is correct.

Medium (9)

Q09. How do log, Box–Cox, and calendar adjustments help a time series model?

Difficulty: Medium · Based on source notes

NoteReference Answer

Transformations can stabilize the scale or variance; calendar adjustments remove predictable variation due to month length, holidays, working days, or population exposure.

TipDetailed Explanation

A shop selling 3,000 units in a 30-day month and 3,100 in a 31-day month may have nearly identical average daily sales. Comparing raw monthly totals can manufacture apparent growth. Daily sales per working day may be the more useful target.

For positive data, logs compress large values and may make multiplicative seasonal swings more stable. The Box–Cox family includes log as the limit near lambda = 0. A transformation is not a guarantee of stationarity, and the transformed forecasts must be mapped back to the original units carefully.

Q10. What is time-series decomposition, and when is an additive versus multiplicative model appropriate?

Difficulty: Medium · Based on source notes

NoteReference Answer

Additive decomposition writes observed values as trend plus seasonal plus remainder. Multiplicative decomposition represents the observed value as their product, often useful when seasonal amplitude grows with the level.

TipDetailed Explanation

With an additive model, December sales may be consistently about 200 above an annual trend. With a multiplicative model, December sales may be consistently 20% above trend—so the absolute seasonal swing grows as the business grows.

The log of a positive multiplicative series converts products to sums and often makes additive-style decomposition convenient. Inspect actual plots before choosing a representation; not every series supports a meaningful multiplicative decomposition (particularly around zero or with negative values).

Q11. What is STL decomposition, and how does it improve on classical decomposition?

Difficulty: Medium · Based on source notes

NoteReference Answer

STL uses locally weighted regression to estimate trend and seasonal components flexibly. Compared with classical fixed-seasonal decomposition, it can accommodate seasonal patterns that change gradually over time and offers robust fitting options.

TipDetailed Explanation

Imagine a shopping website whose holiday peak has grown from 10% above normal demand to 25% above normal demand over five years. Classical decomposition may treat December’s effect as constant. STL can estimate an evolving seasonal component from local neighborhoods of observations.

STL itself is primarily a decomposition technique, not automatically a complete forecasting model. You can forecast the seasonally adjusted remainder/trend using another method, then reconstruct forecasts. With multiple seasonalities (for example hourly plus weekly), consider suitable multi-seasonal approaches.

Q12. What is a centered moving average, and why do you use 2×12 moving averages for monthly data?

Difficulty: Medium · Based on source notes

NoteReference Answer

A centered moving average smooths short-term variation symmetrically around a time point. For an even seasonal period such as 12, applying a two-term moving average to a 12-term moving average centers the estimate.

TipDetailed Explanation

A 12-month average spans an even number of observations, so its center falls between two months. Averaging adjacent 12-month averages produces a 2×12 moving average centered on an actual month.

This helps estimate a trend-cycle for decomposition. However, centered moving averages use future observations relative to the central month; they are fine for retrospective analysis but cause leakage if used as features for real-time forecasting at that same month. Do not confuse a smoothing moving average with the MA(q) stochastic model in ARIMA.

Q13. How do you distinguish stationary from nonstationary processes?

Difficulty: Medium · Based on source notes

NoteReference Answer

A weakly stationary process has a constant mean, finite time-invariant variance, and autocovariance that depends only on lag. A nonstationary process violates at least one of these conditions.

TipDetailed Explanation

A stable AR(1) process fluctuating around a fixed level can be stationary. A random walk usually is not: a shock has a permanent effect, and uncertainty about its level grows over time. A series with deterministic upward trend is also nonstationary in levels, although detrending may create a stationary residual.

Many forecasting approaches can handle nonstationarity directly, including models with explicit trends, differencing, and state-space components. Therefore, stationarity is not a universal prerequisite for forecasting; it is an assumption required for particular modeling and inference tools.

Q14. What is the difference between stochastic trend and deterministic trend?

Difficulty: Medium · Based on source notes

NoteReference Answer

A deterministic trend is a predictable function of time around which disturbances may be stationary. A stochastic trend contains accumulating random shocks, such as a unit root, so simple detrending does not remove it.

TipDetailed Explanation

Consider series A: \(y_t=100+2t+u_t\) with stationary noise \(u_t\). Removing the deterministic line leaves stable fluctuations. Series B: \(y_t=y_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t\) is a random walk. Subtracting a straight line does not remove the accumulation of past shocks.

Using the wrong treatment can distort long-term forecasts. Trend-stationary series may return toward a fixed trajectory after a shock, while a unit-root series can experience a lasting level change.

Q15. What do the ADF and KPSS tests actually test?

Difficulty: Medium · Based on source notes

NoteReference Answer

ADF typically tests the null of a unit root. KPSS tests the null of level or trend stationarity, depending on the specification. Neither test alone proves stationarity or nonstationarity.

TipDetailed Explanation

For ADF, a sufficiently small p-value supports rejecting a unit-root null for the specified deterministic terms and lag structure. KPSS works in the opposite direction: a small p-value challenges the stationarity null. ADF may have low power against near-unit-root behavior; KPSS may reject because of breaks or model misspecification.

Example: If ADF fails to reject while KPSS rejects level stationarity, differencing deserves investigation. But do not automatically difference: first inspect plots, possible deterministic trends, seasonality, structural breaks, and the stationarity assumptions implied by the question.

Python illustration — the null hypotheses go in opposite directions:

import numpy as np
from statsmodels.tsa.stattools import adfuller, kpss

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
shocks = rng.normal(size=300)
random_walk = np.cumsum(shocks)  # Typically I(1)

adf_p = adfuller(random_walk, regression="c")[1]
kpss_p = kpss(random_walk, regression="c", nlags="auto")[1]
print(f"ADF p-value: {adf_p:.3f}")
print(f"KPSS p-value: {kpss_p:.3f}")

ADF asks whether a unit root can be rejected; KPSS asks whether level stationarity can be rejected. A p-value is not the probability the null hypothesis is true. statsmodels may warn that an extreme test statistic lies outside its tabulated p-value range; in that case, the printed p-value is a bound rather than an exact tail probability. Interpretation still depends on the regression specification, sample length, and possible breaks.

Q16. What is the Ljung–Box test and how should you interpret it?

Difficulty: Medium · Based on source notes

NoteReference Answer

The Ljung–Box test evaluates whether a group of autocorrelations up to a chosen lag is jointly zero. A small p-value suggests residual serial correlation; a large p-value means the test did not find sufficient evidence against that null.

TipDetailed Explanation

Suppose a monthly model leaves visible spikes at lags 1 and 12. A Ljung–Box test at a relevant set of lags may identify systematic residual dependence. The lag choice matters, as does accounting for fitted ARMA parameters where appropriate.

There is no universal rule that a p-value must exceed 0.10. Failing to reject does not prove residuals are independent or Gaussian. Examine residual ACF plots, time plots, squared residuals, and forecast errors alongside the test.

Q17. Why can two unrelated trending series have a misleading regression relationship?

Difficulty: Medium · Based on source notes

NoteReference Answer

Common stochastic or deterministic trends can generate high R-squared and apparently significant coefficients even when the variables have no meaningful causal relationship. This is known as spurious regression.

TipDetailed Explanation

Suppose two independent random walks both drift upward over ten years. Regressing one on the other may show a large t-statistic and a high R-squared. Ordinary regression standard errors assume conditions that the nonstationary residuals may violate.

A sensible workflow tests the integration properties, checks domain theory, and distinguishes relationships in levels from relationships in changes. If the variables share a valid cointegrating relationship, a levels term may be meaningful; if not, use appropriate transformations or dynamic models rather than trusting the levels regression.

Hard (4)

Q18. What is the difference between a structural break, a regime switch, and ordinary random noise?

Difficulty: Hard · Added for interview prep

NoteReference Answer

A structural break is a persistent change in model parameters; a regime-switching process moves between distinct states; ordinary noise creates temporary deviations without changing the underlying relationship.

TipDetailed Explanation

A housing-market model trained during years of low mortgage rates may behave differently after a sharp policy-rate increase. A single month’s unusual sales is noise; a long-lasting change in the relationship between rates and purchases is a structural break. A market alternating between high- and low-volatility states may be modeled with regimes.

Diagnostics include rolling coefficients, residual monitoring, breakpoint tests, and out-of-time validation. Reacting to every surprising observation as a structural break leads to overfitting; ignoring durable changes leads to stale forecasts.

Q19. What is conditional heteroskedasticity and when might ARCH/GARCH help?

Difficulty: Hard · Added for interview prep

NoteReference Answer

Conditional heteroskedasticity means the conditional variance changes over time. ARCH/GARCH models describe that changing volatility, often useful for financial returns or other volatility-clustered data.

TipDetailed Explanation

Daily financial returns may have almost no autocorrelation in their mean, yet large moves cluster together. An AR model for the mean does not explain this. A simple GARCH model makes today’s conditional volatility depend on past squared shocks and past volatility.

The key distinction is between predicting the level/mean and predicting the uncertainty/variance. A good mean forecast can still yield poorly calibrated risk intervals if volatility dynamics are ignored.

Q20. Can a series have multiple seasonal periods and why is ordinary SARIMA sometimes insufficient?

Difficulty: Hard · Added for interview prep

NoteReference Answer

Yes. Hourly demand can have daily and weekly patterns simultaneously. A single SARIMA seasonal period may not capture multiple long seasonalities efficiently.

TipDetailed Explanation

Electricity demand at 6 p.m. differs from 3 a.m. and also differs between weekdays and weekends. In hourly data, seasonal periods 24 and 168 both matter. One ordinary SARIMA seasonal component may capture only one of them directly, and high-order seasonal lags can be expensive or unstable.

Alternatives include Fourier features for each relevant period, dynamic harmonic regression, TBATS-like multi-seasonal models, or ML models with carefully engineered calendar features. Evaluate each approach in genuine temporal backtests.

Q21. Why can aggregation change autocorrelation and apparent predictability?

Difficulty: Hard · Added for interview prep

NoteReference Answer

Aggregation changes the scale, signal-to-noise ratio, and dependence structure; monthly averages or totals can behave very differently from the underlying daily series.

TipDetailed Explanation

Suppose daily housing transactions are erratic due to individual closings. Monthly totals smooth some variation, making trends appear stronger; however, fewer time points remain for estimation. Monthly sums also vary with the number of days, while monthly averages do not in the same way.

For multilevel data, aggregation can conceal regional differences or create Simpson’s paradox. Define the forecasting target at the level where the decision is made, and do not assume a model validated at the national level is accurate for individual cities.

Q01. How do MAE, MSE, and RMSE compare?

Difficulty: Easy · Based on source notes

NoteReference Answer

MAE averages absolute errors, MSE averages squared errors, and RMSE is the square root of MSE. Squaring penalizes large mistakes more heavily, while MAE is often easier to interpret.

TipDetailed Explanation

Suppose three weekly forecasts have errors of +1, -2, and +9. MAE is \((1+2+9)/3=4\) units. MSE is \((1+4+81)/3\approx 28.7\) squared units, and RMSE is about 5.35 units.

For a point forecast under squared-error loss, the conditional mean is optimal. Under absolute-error loss, the conditional median is optimal. Therefore, the business cost of a large miss determines whether MAE or RMSE is the more useful headline metric; neither is universally best.

Q02. What is a prediction interval, and how does it differ from a confidence interval?

Difficulty: Easy · Based on source notes

NoteReference Answer

A prediction interval describes uncertainty about a future observation; a confidence interval describes uncertainty about an estimated parameter or conditional mean. Prediction intervals usually must account for future noise as well as estimation uncertainty.

TipDetailed Explanation

Suppose we estimate average next-month appreciation to be 0.3%. A confidence interval might quantify uncertainty about the true expected rate. A 95% prediction interval might instead cover a much wider range, such as -1.2% to +1.8%, because future housing prices can move unpredictably even if the mean is estimated precisely.

Prediction intervals should be evaluated by their coverage: among many comparable forecasts, about 95% of future realizations should land inside nominal 95% intervals, subject to the stated assumptions.

Q03. Why should you not use a random 80/20 split in most forecasting problems?

Difficulty: Easy · Based on source notes

NoteReference Answer

A random split mixes past and future and can leak future information into model training. Use chronologically ordered holdouts and rolling-origin backtesting instead.

TipDetailed Explanation

If your model is supposed to forecast January 2027 using information available in December 2026, it is not valid to train on February 2027 rows while evaluating January 2027. Random k-fold cross-validation frequently does exactly this.

A simple chronological split keeps earlier data for training and later data for testing. Multiple rolling-origin splits are better when you need a robust assessment across different market conditions. Some specialist methods allow other split schemes under strong assumptions, but random k-fold should not be the default for temporal forecasting.

Q04. What is the difference between one-step-ahead and multi-step-ahead forecasting?

Difficulty: Easy · Based on source notes

NoteReference Answer

A one-step forecast predicts the next observation. An h-step forecast predicts h periods into the future; it often faces greater uncertainty and may require forecasting intervening variables.

TipDetailed Explanation

Forecasting next month’s HPA is different from forecasting the next 12 monthly HPA observations. The 12-step forecast may accumulate uncertainty and rely on future paths of interest rates and economic conditions.

A model can score well on one-step-ahead predictions while performing poorly at twelve months. Always align evaluation with the operational horizon, and report errors by horizon rather than treating every prediction as interchangeable.

Q05. What is the difference between ex-ante and ex-post forecasts?

Difficulty: Easy · Based on source notes

NoteReference Answer

An ex-ante forecast uses only information genuinely available at the forecast origin. An ex-post forecast may use later-realized predictor values and is useful for conditional analysis, not an honest real-time performance claim.

TipDetailed Explanation

Suppose you predict next-quarter home prices using next-quarter mortgage rates. In January, you do not know the realized March rate. A forecast using the realized March rate is ex post unless the March value had actually been fixed and known beforehand.

Such a forecast might show how the price model would have performed with perfect rate information, but it overstates a deployed system’s accuracy. For ex-ante testing, forecast the rate separately or specify a rate scenario. This distinction is especially important in economic forecasting.

Medium (8)

Q06. What is expanding-window versus rolling-window cross-validation?

Difficulty: Medium · Based on source notes

NoteReference Answer

Expanding-window validation retains all available past history at each origin. Rolling-window validation keeps a fixed-length recent history and discards older observations.

TipDetailed Explanation

For monthly data: the first expanding split might train on 2015–2020 and validate on 2021; the next trains on 2015–2021 and validates on 2022. A five-year rolling window would instead train on 2017–2021 for the 2022 test.

An expanding window uses more data and can reduce estimation noise. A rolling window may respond better to changes in market structure. Set the origin spacing and window length to mimic how often the model is actually refit in production.

Q07. What is a forecast horizon, and why should the score be reported separately by horizon?

Difficulty: Medium · Based on source notes

NoteReference Answer

The horizon is how far into the future the target lies relative to the information cutoff. The forecast error distribution and the ranking of models can differ by horizon.

TipDetailed Explanation

An AR model may predict the next month’s sales very well from recent observations. A model with economic indicators might be better for twelve-month forecasts. Pooling all errors into one RMSE can hide this difference.

For backtesting, construct a table with forecast origins as rows and horizons as columns. Compare \(h=1\), \(h=3\), \(h=6\), and \(h=12\) separately, then apply business-relevant weighting if a combined metric is required.

Q08. What are MAPE, sMAPE, and MASE, and when can they mislead?

Difficulty: Medium · Based on source notes

NoteReference Answer

MAPE divides absolute error by actual magnitude and is unstable near zero. sMAPE is a modified relative error with its own weaknesses. MASE scales MAE by the in-sample error of a naïve benchmark, enabling comparisons across series.

TipDetailed Explanation

If actual sales are 1 and you forecast 3, the absolute percentage error is 200%. If actual sales are zero, MAPE is undefined. Negative or near-zero targets such as monthly appreciation make MAPE especially troublesome.

MASE uses a training-sample benchmark scale rather than dividing by each realized outcome. It can be more reliable for comparing different markets, although it still requires a sensible denominator and correct seasonal benchmark. For house-price appreciation, MAE in percentage points is often the clearest business metric.

Q09. How do you select the best model when AIC prefers one model but backtest RMSE prefers another?

Difficulty: Medium · Based on source notes

NoteReference Answer

Choose according to the stated goal. AIC estimates relative information loss within a likelihood-based candidate family, whereas out-of-sample RMSE measures actual forecasting performance under squared-error loss.

TipDetailed Explanation

Suppose a SARIMA model has lower AIC on the training data, but an ETS model has consistently lower six-month forecast RMSE across several validation origins. If six-month accuracy is the business objective, the ETS model is the stronger choice.

AIC, AICc, and BIC can narrow candidate specifications without costly full backtests. They are not substitutes for honest external validation, and should not be compared mechanically across models fit to incomparable transformed targets or data windows.

Q10. Why are AICc and BIC used, and how do they differ from AIC?

Difficulty: Medium · Based on source notes

NoteReference Answer

AIC penalizes model complexity; AICc adds a finite-sample correction; BIC generally penalizes additional parameters more heavily as sample size grows.

TipDetailed Explanation

ARIMA(2,1,2) can fit training data better than ARIMA(1,1,0), but its extra parameters risk fitting noise. Information criteria trade off fit and complexity. AICc is often preferred when the sample is small relative to the number of fitted parameters.

BIC may select a simpler model, especially with many observations. None guarantees the best forecast under the target horizon and loss. Use comparable likelihoods and appropriate sample definitions, then backtest the shortlisted models.

Q11. How do you inspect whether a forecasting model has left useful signal in its residuals?

Difficulty: Medium · Based on source notes

NoteReference Answer

Check residuals over time, their mean and spread, the ACF, and relevant portmanteau tests such as Ljung–Box. Also examine large errors by season and forecast horizon.

TipDetailed Explanation

If residual ACF spikes at lag 12 in monthly data, the model probably missed annual seasonality. A persistent positive residual mean means the model tends to underpredict. Outliers confined to recession months suggest structural instability.

The ideal mean-model residual is unpredictable, not necessarily perfectly Gaussian. Normality and constant variance can be useful for simple analytic intervals, but well-calibrated intervals may require modeling conditional volatility, bootstrap procedures, or robust methods.

Q12. How do residual bootstrap prediction intervals work?

Difficulty: Medium · Based on source notes

NoteReference Answer

Draw shocks from appropriately diagnosed historical residuals, simulate future paths recursively, and take quantiles of the simulated outcomes at each horizon.

TipDetailed Explanation

Imagine forecasting twelve months of sales. First fit the model on historical data and obtain residuals. For each simulated future, randomly sample plausible future shocks and feed them through the model’s recursion. Repeat this many times, then take, for example, the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles at each horizon.

This method assumes sampled historical residuals represent future shocks sufficiently well. If residuals are autocorrelated, heteroskedastic, or structurally changing, naïve independent residual sampling can be wrong. Use a bootstrap design that respects the model and temporal dependence when needed.

Q13. Why is taking the exponential of a log forecast not always an unbiased level forecast?

Difficulty: Medium · Based on source notes

NoteReference Answer

Exponentiating an estimate of the conditional mean of log Y typically gives a conditional median or geometric-mean-type prediction, not the arithmetic conditional mean of Y. A retransformation adjustment may be needed.

TipDetailed Explanation

If \(Z=\log Y\) is normally distributed with conditional mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\), then \(\exp(\mu)\) is the conditional median of \(Y\), while \(E[Y]=\exp(\mu+\sigma^2/2)\). With variance 0.04, the arithmetic mean is about 2% above the naïve exponentiated log mean.

For house-price forecasts, be explicit whether your target is the mean index level, median scenario, or a quantile. Bias corrections must match the assumed error distribution; smearing estimators are another option.

Hard (7)

Q14. What are probabilistic calibration, interval coverage, and sharpness?

Difficulty: Hard · Added for interview prep

NoteReference Answer

Calibration asks whether forecasted probabilities match realized frequencies; coverage asks how often realized values fall inside intervals; sharpness rewards intervals that are narrow subject to adequate calibration.

TipDetailed Explanation

If you issue 100 nominal 90% prediction intervals, around 90 future outcomes should be covered in the long run. An interval from negative infinity to positive infinity has excellent coverage but zero usefulness. That is why coverage must be assessed alongside average interval width.

Plot empirical coverage by horizon and region. A model can be well calibrated for one-month forecasts but severely overconfident at twelve months, especially during recessions or housing-market reversals.

Q15. What are quantile forecasts and pinball loss?

Difficulty: Hard · Added for interview prep

NoteReference Answer

A quantile forecast targets a specified conditional percentile. Pinball loss evaluates quantile forecasts by asymmetrically penalizing errors above and below the predicted quantile.

TipDetailed Explanation

For risk management, the 10th percentile of next year’s housing-price growth may matter more than the average. Pinball loss for quantile level \(\tau\) is \(\rho_\tau(e)=e(\tau-\mathbf{1}\{e<0\})\), where \(e=y-\hat q_\tau\).

At \(\tau=0.9\), underpredicting the realized outcome gets more weight than overpredicting. Different quantile targets serve different decisions. When forecasting multiple percentiles, also check that lower forecast quantiles never exceed higher forecast quantiles.

Q16. What is the Diebold–Mariano test, and when would you use it?

Difficulty: Hard · Added for interview prep

NoteReference Answer

The Diebold–Mariano test compares expected predictive loss between two forecast methods using a sequence of paired forecast loss differences, with dependence-aware variance estimation.

TipDetailed Explanation

Suppose ARIMA and XGBoost each forecast the same 60 months. Compute monthly differences in squared errors, such as \(d_t=e^2_{ARIMA,t}-e^2_{XGB,t}\). The null is that the mean loss difference is zero under appropriate regularity conditions.

The loss differential can be serially correlated, especially with overlapping multi-step forecasts, so a plain independent-sample t-test is often inappropriate. A statistically significant result is not automatically economically important. Check whether one model’s gain justifies extra complexity and maintenance.

Q17. How should you construct prediction intervals with a known lower bound such as zero?

Difficulty: Hard · Added for interview prep

NoteReference Answer

Use a modeling strategy consistent with the support of the target: log or other appropriate transforms, count distributions, constrained forecasts, or simulation models. Do not rely solely on clipping negative predictions to zero.

TipDetailed Explanation

Sales and mortgage prepayments cannot be negative. A normal interval around a low mean might extend below zero. A log-scale model can enforce positive predictions, but cannot represent true zeros without an additional strategy. Count models such as Poisson or negative binomial may be appropriate for counts, while zero-inflated or hurdle models can handle excess zeros.

Clipping intervals post hoc may destroy nominal coverage. Evaluate realistic constraints and calibration after any transformation or correction.

Q18. How do model combination and forecast ensembles improve robustness?

Difficulty: Hard · Added for interview prep

NoteReference Answer

Combining forecasts can reduce dependence on any single model’s errors, especially when constituent models make different mistakes. Simple averages are strong baselines but improvement is not guaranteed.

TipDetailed Explanation

A seasonal naïve model may respond quickly to recent movements; a dynamic regression may respond to macroeconomic scenarios; a gradient-boosted model may capture nonlinearities. An average can be more stable than any individual forecast if their errors are sufficiently different.

A common mistake is optimizing ensemble weights on the final test period. Tune weights on earlier rolling-origin validation, then assess the locked combination on a genuinely later holdout. Track interval calibration too: combining point forecasts does not automatically produce valid predictive intervals.

Q19. What are conformal prediction intervals for time series, and what is the main challenge?

Difficulty: Hard · Added for interview prep

NoteReference Answer

Conformal prediction calibrates intervals using empirical forecast errors. Standard exchangeability-based finite-sample guarantees do not directly hold for serially dependent and nonstationary time series.

TipDetailed Explanation

You can train a model on older data, collect absolute errors on a later calibration window, then add a suitable high quantile of those errors around new predictions. This is intuitive and model-agnostic.

However, forecasting errors may change across seasons, horizons, and volatility regimes. Methods for time series may use rolling calibration, horizon-specific scores, or dependence-aware assumptions. Do not claim an exact 90% finite-sample marginal guarantee merely because you used ordinary conformal prediction on dependent observations.

Q20. What is a Fourier term, and how can it model long seasonal patterns?

Difficulty: Hard · Added for interview prep

NoteReference Answer

Fourier terms are sine and cosine functions with specified periods and frequencies. They approximate smooth repeating seasonal patterns without requiring many seasonal dummy or autoregressive coefficients.

Q01. What is an autoregressive AR(p) model?

Difficulty: Easy · Based on source notes

NoteReference Answer

An AR(p) model predicts the current value using p lagged values of the same series and a new disturbance. For stationarity, the autoregressive parameters must satisfy appropriate root conditions.

TipDetailed Explanation

The simplest example is \(y_t=c+\phi y_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t\). If \(\phi=0.7\), a value that is temporarily high tends to remain partly high next period, but the deviation decays over time. If \(\phi=1\), the model is a random walk rather than a stable mean-reverting AR(1).

Q02. What is an MA(q) model, and how is it different from moving-average smoothing?

Difficulty: Easy · Based on source notes

NoteReference Answer

An MA(q) stochastic model expresses the current value as a mean plus current and q past random shocks. Moving-average smoothing instead averages observed data points to reveal a trend.

TipDetailed Explanation

An MA(1) can be written \(y_t=\mu+\varepsilon_t+\theta\varepsilon_{t-1}\). Last month’s unexpected shock still affects this month’s outcome, but older shocks do not enter directly. In a 12-month moving-average smoother, we average observations from a 12-month window.

The two techniques share a name but model different objects: one uses unobserved innovations, the other observed values. This is among the most frequent ARIMA interview traps.

Q03. What do the three numbers in ARIMA(p,d,q) mean?

Difficulty: Easy · Based on source notes

NoteReference Answer

p is the autoregressive order, d is the number of ordinary differences, and q is the moving-average innovation order. ARMA dynamics are modeled for the differenced series.

TipDetailed Explanation

For ARIMA(1,1,1), first calculate \(\Delta y_t=y_t-y_{t-1}\). Then model that differenced series using one autoregressive lag and one moving-average lag of innovations.

Differencing targets stochastic trends; it does not automatically remove changing seasonality or variance instability. Values of d above 1 are sometimes appropriate, but excess differencing usually harms interpretability and forecasting accuracy.

Q04. How do ordinary differencing and seasonal differencing differ?

Difficulty: Easy · Based on source notes

NoteReference Answer

Ordinary differencing subtracts the previous observation: \(\Delta y_t=y_t-y_{t-1}\). Seasonal differencing subtracts the observation one seasonal period earlier: \(\Delta_m y_t=y_t-y_{t-m}\).

TipDetailed Explanation

If monthly December sales repeatedly exceed November sales, ordinary differencing may still preserve much of the annual seasonal dependence. Seasonal differencing with \(m=12\) compares this December directly with last December.

For a series with both a stochastic trend and seasonal unit roots, you may need both types. But automatic repeated differencing is dangerous: diagnose whether remaining patterns are stochastic seasonality, deterministic calendar effects, or breaks.

Q05. What does SARIMA(p,d,q)(P,D,Q)m mean?

SARIMA extends ARIMA with seasonal autoregressive order P, seasonal differencing D, seasonal moving-average order Q, and seasonal period m.

TipDetailed Explanation

The uppercase seasonal terms operate at multiples of the seasonal lag. For example, a seasonal MA(1) at \(m=12\) incorporates the innovation from twelve months earlier.

Q06. What is a random walk, and what forecast does it imply?

Difficulty: Easy · Based on source notes

NoteReference Answer

A random walk satisfies \(y_t=y_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t\); with zero-mean shocks, its conditional point forecast is the latest observed value for every future horizon.

TipDetailed Explanation

Imagine an index currently at 240. If shocks average zero and there is no drift, the best mean forecast of the level for next month or six months later is 240. That does not mean six-month outcomes are tightly concentrated around 240—uncertainty typically grows with the horizon.

A random walk with drift, \(y_t=c+y_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t\), instead forecasts \(y_{T+h}\) as \(y_T+hc\). The drift must be estimated carefully and can be unstable under structural changes.

Q07. How are the ACF and PACF used to select candidate AR and MA orders?

TipDetailed Explanation

Q08. Why do ARIMA forecasts set unknown future innovations to zero?

Difficulty: Medium · Based on source notes

NoteReference Answer

For a mean forecast, unknown future innovations are replaced by their conditional expectation, which is zero under the correctly specified innovation model.

TipDetailed Explanation

For prediction intervals or scenario simulation, however, you should not set shocks to zero. Instead, represent their uncertainty and propagate it through the model. Point forecasting and uncertainty forecasting answer different questions.

Q09. What are invertibility and stationarity in ARMA models?

Difficulty: Medium · Based on source notes

NoteReference Answer

Stationarity controls the long-run behavior of the autoregressive component. Invertibility ensures the moving-average innovations can be recovered consistently from the observed process; both involve polynomial root conditions.

TipDetailed Explanation

For AR(1), \(|\phi|<1\) ensures weak stationarity under standard conditions. For MA(1), \(|\theta|<1\) is an invertibility condition under the common \(1+\theta B\) convention. Q10. How do you choose p, d, and q systematically?

Difficulty: Medium · Based on source notes

NoteReference Answer

TipDetailed Explanation

A practical sequence is: (1) plot the series, (2) consider transformations or calendar adjustments, (3) choose d using plots, domain knowledge, and tests such as ADF/KPSS, (4) propose candidate orders via ACF/PACF, (5) use AICc for comparable candidates, and (6) run rolling-origin backtests.

Q12. What is dynamic regression with ARIMA errors?

Q13. How are intervention variables and distributed lags used in time-series regression?

Q14. What is an ARIMAX or SARIMAX model, and what is the practical challenge with future X variables?

Difficulty: Medium · Based on source notes

NoteReference Answer

ARIMAX/SARIMAX adds exogenous regressors to ARIMA/SARIMA dynamics. Forecasts require future values of the regressors or explicit scenarios for them.

TipDetailed Explanation

To forecast home prices for the next six months, SARIMAX might include mortgage rates and unemployment. If those future rates are unknown, you must forecast them, condition on scenarios, or restrict the model to predictors available at the decision time.

A common data leakage error is to use realized future unemployment in backtests, while claiming the forecast was issued six months earlier. Store predictor vintages or reconstruct the data that existed at each forecast origin.

Python example — supplying future exogenous values:

import numpy as np
from statsmodels.tsa.statespace.sarimax import SARIMAX

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
months = np.arange(144)
rate = 4.0 + 0.5 * np.sin(months / 9)
sales = (100 + 2 * rate + 8 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * months / 12)
         + rng.normal(0, 3, size=144))

model = SARIMAX(sales, exog=rate[:, None],
                order=(1, 0, 0), seasonal_order=(1, 0, 0, 12),
                trend="c", enforce_stationarity=False,
                enforce_invertibility=False)
fit = model.fit(disp=False, maxiter=250)
future_rate_scenario = np.array([[4.1], [4.0], [3.9]])
forecast = fit.get_forecast(steps=3, exog=future_rate_scenario)
print(forecast.predicted_mean)

The rate values here are assumed scenarios, not known future observations. In a historical backtest, replace them with scenarios or forecasts that really existed at that historical origin.

Q17. How can over-differencing harm ARIMA forecasts?

NoteReference Answer

Over-differencing can inject unnecessary moving-average structure, increase forecast uncertainty, and discard informative level relationships.

Q18. How do ARIMA and exponential smoothing (ETS) compare?

Q19. What is a state-space model, and why is the Kalman filter useful?

Q20. When would you use direct versus recursive multi-step forecasting?

Q21. What is the difference between ARIMA innovations and parameter uncertainty?

Difficulty: Hard · Added for interview prep

NoteReference Answer

Innovations are unforeseeable future shocks under the model; parameter uncertainty comes from estimating unknown model coefficients from finite data. Both can influence forecast uncertainty.

TipDetailed Explanation

Even if you knew AR(1) coefficient \(\phi\) exactly, next month’s random shock would create predictive spread. If you estimated \(\phi\) from just 24 months of data, different plausible values of \(\phi\) would yield different long-term forecasts.

Q01. What is a unit root and why does it matter in economic time series?

Difficulty: Easy · Based on source notes

NoteReference Answer

A unit root is an autoregressive root on the unit circle, typically yielding a stochastic trend. Shocks can have persistent effects, and standard stationary-model inference may be invalid in levels.

TipDetailed Explanation

For \(y_t=\phi y_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t\), \(\phi=1\) produces a random walk: a one-time positive shock shifts the entire future path. With \(|\phi|<1\), the effect dies out over time under standard assumptions. With \(|\phi|>1\), the simple AR(1) is explosive.

Do not confuse a unit root with an unusually high but stationary persistence such as \(\phi=0.98\). Distinguishing them can be difficult in short macroeconomic samples, and the forecast implications are very different.

Q02. What does I(1) mean, and how is it different from I(0)?

Difficulty: Easy · Based on source notes

NoteReference Answer

An I(0) series is stationary under the relevant assumptions; an I(1) series is nonstationary in levels but becomes I(0) after first differencing.

TipDetailed Explanation

The log of a house-price index might be approximately I(1) over some period, while its monthly log growth can be closer to I(0). This is an empirical approximation, not a universal law: structural breaks and evolving volatility complicate the classification.

Economists distinguish these orders because combining unrelated I(1) series in an ordinary levels regression can produce spurious correlations, while combining cointegrated I(1) variables may reveal meaningful long-run relations.

Q03. What is cointegration?

Difficulty: Easy · Based on source notes

NoteReference Answer

Two or more nonstationary variables are cointegrated if some nontrivial linear combination of them is stationary, indicating a stable long-run relationship under suitable assumptions.

TipDetailed Explanation

Suppose log house prices and log household income both contain stochastic trends. If \(u_t=\log(HPI_t)-\beta\log(Income_t)\) is stationary, their relative long-run deviation tends to revert, even though the levels themselves may not.

Cointegration is stronger than correlation and weaker than causation. It can motivate an error-correction model, but it does not establish that income causally drives home prices or identify the direction of adjustment without further assumptions.

Q04. What is an error-correction model (ECM)?

Difficulty: Easy · Based on source notes

NoteReference Answer

An ECM models short-run changes in a variable while including a lagged deviation from a long-run equilibrium. The error-correction term links temporary movements with longer-run adjustment.

TipDetailed Explanation

Suppose long-run house prices depend on income and mortgage rates. If prices are temporarily above the fitted equilibrium, next month’s price growth may be lower as the gap closes. A typical ECM includes lagged growth, changes in economic indicators, and the previous period’s equilibrium residual.

In a common sign convention, a negative adjustment coefficient indicates correction when prices are above equilibrium. Its sign is not a mathematical law; it depends on how you define the gap, which variable adjusts, and whether the relationship is stable.

Q05. What is the Engle–Granger two-step approach?

Q07. Is the error-correction coefficient always negative?

Q09. What is Granger causality, and what does it not establish?

Q11. How does a VECM differ from a VAR in differences?

Q17. What is the difference between a structural VAR and a reduced-form VAR?

Difficulty: Hard · Added for interview prep

NoteReference Answer

A reduced-form VAR models statistical innovations, which can be contemporaneously correlated. A structural VAR imposes identifying restrictions to interpret particular innovations as economically meaningful shocks.

TipDetailed Explanation

Suppose the inflation and policy-rate equations both react to unexpected news at the same time. The residuals from the reduced-form VAR mix several underlying economic disturbances. To speak of a monetary-policy shock, researchers may impose ordering restrictions, sign restrictions, external instruments, or other identifying assumptions.

Changing these assumptions can change impulse responses substantially. Predictive usefulness of the VAR is separate from identification of a causal shock.

Q18. What are impulse responses and forecast error variance decomposition?

Difficulty: Hard · Added for interview prep

NoteReference Answer

An impulse response traces a variable’s dynamic reaction to a specified innovation in a VAR or related model. Forecast error variance decomposition attributes portions of forecast uncertainty to different identified shocks.

TipDetailed Explanation

An impulse response might show how log house prices respond over 24 months to a one-unit policy-rate innovation. The shape depends on model parameters and shock identification. A reduced-form residual shock is not automatically a clean policy intervention.

Variance decomposition may indicate, under a particular identification, that interest-rate shocks explain 20% of one-year forecast variation. This is a model-dependent statistical allocation, not a universal causal fact.

Q19. Why can cointegration fail during a structural break, and what should you do?

Difficulty: Hard · Added for interview prep

NoteReference Answer

A previously stable long-run combination can change after structural shifts in policy, technology, demographics, or market institutions. Standard constant-parameter tests may be misleading around breaks.

TipDetailed Explanation

A house-price/income relation estimated before a large shift in mortgage underwriting may not apply afterward. Residuals may look mean-reverting in the historical estimation period but behave persistently after the policy change.

Inspect rolling residuals and parameter stability, test for breaks where appropriate, and backtest across distinct regimes. A regime-sensitive model, a revised equilibrium relation, or scenario-specific forecasts may be better than mechanically extrapolating a decades-old cointegration vector.

Q20. How do panel time-series forecasts differ from one national aggregate series?

Difficulty: Hard · Added for interview prep

NoteReference Answer

Panel forecasting involves multiple entities observed over time. Shared factors, local heterogeneity, cross-sectional dependence, and hierarchical aggregation matter in addition to temporal dynamics.

TipDetailed Explanation

If you forecast HPA for 50 states, their errors may be correlated because all respond to national mortgage-rate shocks. Treating each state as independent understates uncertainty. State-specific price growth can also behave differently from the national aggregate because populations, housing supply, and weights differ.

A panel or hierarchical model may share strength across regions while allowing local coefficients. If national forecasts must equal a weighted aggregate of state forecasts, forecast reconciliation should be part of evaluation.

Q21. Why are standard OLS t-tests sometimes invalid in macroeconomic time-series regressions?

Difficulty: Hard · Added for interview prep

NoteReference Answer

Serially correlated errors, heteroskedasticity, nonstationary regressors, cointegration issues, and model-selection uncertainty can invalidate ordinary iid standard errors and conventional asymptotics.

TipDetailed Explanation

Regressing quarterly output growth on policy rates with autocorrelated disturbances can produce underestimated standard errors if you treat each quarter as independent. HAC/Newey–West standard errors can address suitable heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation conditions in stationary regression settings.

HAC adjustment cannot rescue a spurious regression of unrelated I(1) levels, identify a causal policy effect, or fix serious omitted-variable bias. Diagnose the stochastic specification first, then choose inference appropriate to the actual estimator.

Q22. What is nowcasting, and how do you combine data observed at different frequencies?

Difficulty: Hard · Added for interview prep

NoteReference Answer

Nowcasting estimates the current or very recent state before official data are published. Mixed-frequency models use higher-frequency indicators such as weekly claims or monthly employment to estimate quarterly outcomes.

TipDetailed Explanation

Quarterly GDP becomes available with a lag, but monthly industrial production and weekly unemployment claims arrive sooner. A nowcast can update the current-quarter GDP estimate whenever new information is released.

Methods include mixed-frequency dynamic factor models, MIDAS regressions, bridge equations, and state-space filtering. Unlike converting quarterly GDP to monthly values by simple interpolation, these methods exploit observed higher-frequency indicators and explicitly model information timing. Real-time vintage evaluation is essential: the biggest mistake is to nowcast using revised or unpublished data.

Q23. What are local projections, and how do they differ from VAR impulse responses?

Difficulty: Hard · Added for interview prep

NoteReference Answer

Local projections estimate a separate regression for each future horizon to trace a response to an identified shock. VAR impulse responses derive horizon paths recursively from a joint dynamic model.

TipDetailed Explanation

Suppose an instrument identifies an exogenous monetary-policy shock at time t. A local projection can regress GDP growth from t to t+h on the shock plus lagged controls separately for h = 0, 1, …, 12. The resulting coefficients trace the estimated dynamic response.

Local projections can be flexible and robust to some forms of dynamic misspecification but may be noisier at long horizons. Neither method creates causal identification on its own: credible shock identification and appropriate controls remain essential.

Q01. How can XGBoost or random forests be used for time-series forecasting?

Difficulty: Easy · Based on source notes

NoteReference Answer

Convert time series into a supervised-learning table using lagged targets, lagged predictors, calendar features, and other information known at each forecast origin. Fit the model using strictly time-ordered training and validation.

TipDetailed Explanation

To predict next month’s sales, make features such as sales one, two, and twelve months ago; last month’s advertising; the target month’s known calendar month; and economic forecasts available today. A gradient-boosted tree can learn nonlinear interactions, such as strong holiday effects for certain stores.

The algorithm itself does not enforce chronology. A carefully constructed feature table, realistic backtesting, and timestamp-aware preprocessing are essential. Tree models also do not necessarily extrapolate sustained growth trends well outside the training target range.

Q02. What is a neural-network autoregression (NNAR)?

Difficulty: Easy · Based on source notes

NoteReference Answer

An NNAR uses lagged observations, and sometimes seasonal lags, as inputs to a neural network that forecasts future values.

TipDetailed Explanation

An NNAR with inputs from \(y_{t-1}\), \(y_{t-2}\), \(y_{t-3}\), and \(y_{t-12}\) can learn nonlinear relationships involving recent movements and the same month last year. The hidden layers transform those lagged inputs before outputting a forecast.

Unlike linear AR models, the response need not be a fixed linear weighted sum. This flexibility can help when patterns are nonlinear, but also increases the need for data, regularization, and honest validation. Neural networks are not automatically superior to seasonal naïve or ARIMA baselines.

Q03. Why are holidays and known calendar variables useful forecast features?

Difficulty: Easy · Based on source notes

NoteReference Answer

They represent predictable changes in demand that can be known before the forecast is issued, without guessing future observations of other economic variables.

TipDetailed Explanation

A hotel can know that Thanksgiving will occur within a forecast horizon, even though it does not know future hotel bookings. Calendar features can encode weekends, holidays, payday timing, month-end, trading days, and school calendars.

Holiday effects can shift between dates or differ by region. A simple fixed-month dummy may miss those changes. Make sure the production forecasting horizon actually includes known values of the calendar variable.

Q04. What is the distinction between missing data and a genuine zero in a time series?

Difficulty: Easy · Based on source notes

NoteReference Answer

A zero is an observed numeric outcome; missing means no reliable observation is available. They should be handled differently, especially for sales and transaction series.

TipDetailed Explanation

If a store is open and sells nothing, sales equal zero. If the payment system fails and sales are unrecorded, the value is missing. Filling missing with zero creates a false collapse; interpolating a genuine zero removes a real event.

Check logging uptime, holiday closures, unit definitions, late-arriving data, and publication revisions before imputation. Some time-series state-space models can treat missing observations explicitly without filling them with invented values.

Medium (10)

Q05. How does recursive forecasting differ from a lagged-feature ML model evaluated with actual future lag values?

Difficulty: Medium · Based on source notes

NoteReference Answer

A valid recursive forecast uses earlier predicted values once future lags are no longer observed. Evaluating with realized future target lags answers an easier, different one-step problem.

TipDetailed Explanation

Suppose you need a six-month forecast on January 1. For February, January’s actual outcome is not yet known, so you cannot insert it as a feature if the entire path is issued on January 1. You must use your prediction of January, along with other known information, to forecast February.

A backtest that refreshes with each newly observed monthly value is a rolling one-step forecast, not a fixed-origin six-month projection. Both are valid tasks, but they must be labeled correctly.

Q06. What kinds of data leakage are especially dangerous in time-series machine learning?

Difficulty: Medium · Added for interview prep

NoteReference Answer

Common leaks include centered rolling averages, future-looking target encodings, scalers fit on all dates, revised economic data, future covariate realizations, and overlapping target windows across train and test.

TipDetailed Explanation

If you use a 3-month centered moving average for March, that calculation includes April data and is unavailable when predicting March in real time. Similarly, a 2025 mortgage-rate series downloaded today may include later revisions that were not public in 2025.

Build features relative to each cutoff using publication timestamps, not merely observation dates. In each validation fold, fit imputers, transforms, selectors, and model hyperparameters using only the training period. If labels overlap in time, add an appropriate embargo or gap when justified.

Q07. What is the difference between target date, feature date, and publication date?

Difficulty: Medium · Added for interview prep

NoteReference Answer

The target date is when the outcome occurs; the feature observation date is what period an input describes; publication date is when that input actually became available to forecasters.

TipDetailed Explanation

An unemployment statistic describing March might be published in early April and revised later. An economic forecast made on March 31 cannot use the initially published April value, even though the feature is labeled March.

In production macroeconomic forecasting, maintain data vintages as of each forecast origin. Revision-aware backtests are often materially worse but more honest than backtests built from finalized historical datasets.

Q08. How should you monitor a deployed time-series forecast model?

Difficulty: Medium · Added for interview prep

NoteReference Answer

Monitor forecast accuracy by origin and horizon, missingness and data timeliness, feature drift, interval calibration, residual autocorrelation, and degradation across markets or regimes.

TipDetailed Explanation

For monthly housing forecasts, log the prediction before the future month is observed. Later compare actual appreciation to the stored forecast and record MAE by horizon and MSA. Track whether confidence or prediction intervals cover the realized values and whether large failures cluster around interest-rate changes.

A production system also needs logging, reproducible model versions, input checks, safe retraining procedures, and rollback criteria. Tracking only training RMSE can miss a complete production failure.

Q09. Interview case: How would you forecast monthly home-price appreciation (HPA)?

Difficulty: Medium · Based on source notes

NoteReference Answer

Define target and horizon, benchmark naïve forecasts, inspect history and publication lags, model house-price dynamics alongside macroeconomic predictors, compare ARIMA/ECM and ML candidates, and backtest at realistic forecast origins.

TipDetailed Explanation

First define \(HPA_t=\log(HPI_t)-\log(HPI_{t-1})\) or simple percentage growth and document which one the model predicts. Next inspect seasonality, trends, revisions, missing months, and regional heterogeneity. Fit seasonal naïve and ARIMA baselines. Consider dynamic regression or ECM using mortgage rates, income, employment, supply, and prices when theory and data justify them.

Backtest one-, three-, six-, and twelve-month forecasts using only available predictor vintages. Evaluate MAE, bias, interval coverage, and performance during rate shocks. If modeling nationally and by MSA, assess hierarchical coherence and whether market-specific models improve outcomes.

Q10. Interview case: How would you forecast the distribution of future house prices rather than only the expected HPA?

Difficulty: Medium · Based on source notes

NoteReference Answer

Model the conditional outcome distribution or simulate paths using dynamic equations, innovation shocks, and uncertain macroeconomic scenarios; summarize path quantiles and validate predictive coverage.

TipDetailed Explanation

Start from a known \(HPI_T\). For each month draw a growth shock from a specified model and update \(HPI_{T+h}=HPI_{T+h-1}\exp(HPA_{T+h})\) when HPA is log growth. Continue through the horizon for each simulated path.

If unemployment and mortgage rates are uncertain, draw plausible joint scenarios too; otherwise your house-price fan chart understates macro uncertainty. Evaluate whether simulated 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles agree with subsequently realized outcomes across many historical backtests.

Q11. Interview case: Your ADF test fails to reject a unit root, but KPSS also fails to reject stationarity. What does that imply?

Difficulty: Medium · Based on source notes

NoteReference Answer

The evidence is inconclusive, not proof of both competing claims. The tests have different nulls, power, and sensitivities. Inspect plots, sample length, deterministic trends, seasonal structure, and potential breaks.

TipDetailed Explanation

An ADF p-value of 0.20 means the sample is insufficient to reject its specified unit-root null. A KPSS p-value of 0.12 means insufficient evidence to reject its specified stationarity null. Both results can occur with a short, highly persistent series.

Next compare forecasts under plausible level-stationary, trend-stationary, and integrated specifications. Avoid choosing a model solely by a rigid decision tree of p-values.

Q12. Interview case: An ARIMA model has lower AIC than XGBoost but worse 12-month RMSE. Which model should you deploy?

Difficulty: Medium · Added for interview prep

NoteReference Answer

If the business decision genuinely uses twelve-month forecasts and the validation design is reliable, prioritize the model with better twelve-month out-of-sample performance, subject to stability, uncertainty, latency, and maintainability.

TipDetailed Explanation

AIC is not a universal head-to-head score for unrelated model classes, especially when objective functions differ. XGBoost might capture changing macroeconomic nonlinearities but can also be more fragile under structural breaks.

Check whether the RMSE gain repeats across origins and regions, and whether XGBoost was given future information accidentally. A two-model ensemble or simpler ARIMA may still be preferable if the accuracy gain is small and deployment complexity is high.

Q13. Interview case: How do you forecast thousands of related series with little data in each one?

Difficulty: Medium · Added for interview prep

NoteReference Answer

Use shared models or hierarchical methods that pool information across series, while retaining local identifiers, seasonal patterns, and reconciliation where aggregate consistency matters.

TipDetailed Explanation

Thousands of ZIP-code house-price series may each have only a few years of reliable monthly data. Fitting a large ARIMA separately to each can overfit. A global gradient-boosted model can learn shared effects from interest rates and seasonality with ZIP-level or region-level features.

But shared models may wash out local shocks. Backtest separately for high- and low-volume markets, cold-start regions, and volatile regions. If state forecasts are required to sum coherently to a national forecast, use explicit reconciliation rather than assuming it will happen automatically.

Q14. Interview case: A forecasting model suddenly becomes biased after a large interest-rate shock. What would you do?

Difficulty: Medium · Added for interview prep

NoteReference Answer

First verify the data pipeline and target revisions; then assess structural break, changing covariate effects, and forecast horizon. Consider updating model parameters, adding scenarios, or deploying a fallback baseline.

TipDetailed Explanation

A mortgage shock can change housing affordability and transaction volume faster than an old model expects. A persistent positive residual means actual outcomes exceed forecasts, but the cause could be a new economic regime or simply a broken data feed.

Separate pipeline failure from model misspecification. Compare performance of a refit model, rolling-window model, and simple baseline on dates after the shock. Document why and when the new method should replace the old one; do not silently retrain on the same final test window you use for evaluation.

Q15. What are intermittent-demand forecasting methods and why may ordinary ARIMA struggle?

Difficulty: Hard · Added for interview prep

NoteReference Answer

Intermittent demand has many zero periods interrupted by positive events. Methods such as Croston-family approaches, hurdle models, or count models can be more appropriate than a smooth Gaussian ARIMA.

TipDetailed Explanation

A rare spare part may sell 0 units most days and 5 units on a few days. A standard ARIMA can yield negative forecasts or a smoothed fractional mean that poorly represents inventory risk. Croston-type methods separately smooth demand size and the interval between nonzero demand events.

The relevant decision may be a stockout probability or required inventory quantile rather than the next point forecast. Evaluate demand during stockout periods carefully: observed zero sales can reflect unavailable inventory, not lack of customer demand.

Q16. How would you use backtesting to evaluate a recession stress scenario?

Difficulty: Hard · Added for interview prep

NoteReference Answer

Distinguish conditional stress projections from unconditional forecasts. Assess how the model maps economically plausible recession paths into outcomes, while evaluating predictive calibration and sensitivity to historical regimes.

TipDetailed Explanation

For a stress test, the unemployment and rates path may be imposed, not forecast as the most likely future. The goal is to compute a conditional outcome under that path, such as expected loan losses or home-price declines.

You can compare model responses during previous downturns, examine whether effects are monotonic where theory suggests, and test alternative scenario severities. Do not report a stress-test error as if the model was given real-time ex-ante inputs: it is conditional on assumptions that can be very different from reality.

Q17. Why is cointegration not enough to justify a causal policy simulation?

Difficulty: Hard · Added for interview prep

NoteReference Answer

Cointegration describes a stable long-run statistical combination, not identification of what happens under an intervention. Causal claims require additional assumptions about simultaneous equations, exogeneity, and policy responses.

TipDetailed Explanation

House prices and mortgage rates could be statistically related in the long run, while both react to inflation expectations and monetary policy. If you change rates exogenously, house prices may react differently than they do following ordinary historical rate movements.

For policy scenarios, clearly state whether the model is predictive/conditional, or identify the relevant structural response with an instrument, natural experiment, or structural macroeconomic assumptions. Economists particularly value this distinction in interviews.

Q18. Can gradient-boosted trees extrapolate an upward economic trend beyond the training period?

Difficulty: Hard · Added for interview prep

NoteReference Answer

Many standard tree ensembles make piecewise-constant predictions over feature regions and do not naturally extrapolate a linear trend beyond observed target ranges. Explicit trend modeling or alternative architectures may be needed.

TipDetailed Explanation

If house prices have increased steadily beyond any levels present in training data, a tree model fed only a time index may keep forecasting approximately the largest historical region’s average rather than continuing the slope. An ARIMA with drift or regression with a specified trend can extrapolate, although that extrapolation may itself be wrong after a regime change.

A common hybrid strategy forecasts a trend with a parametric component and lets the tree model predict residual patterns. Validate on later dates where extrapolation genuinely occurs.

Q19. How would you implement rolling-origin validation in Python without leaking future observations?

Difficulty: Hard · Added for interview prep

NoteReference Answer

For each cutoff, fit the model and all training-dependent preprocessing on earlier observations only, predict the designated future horizon, and compare the predictions to later realized values.

TipDetailed Explanation

For monthly data, choose origins such as January, April, July, and October. At each origin, create lagged features from information available by that date, fit the estimator on historical target/feature pairs, and predict the horizon. Keep a record of origin, target date, horizon, and model version.

In scikit-learn, TimeSeriesSplit can create ordered splits for approximately equally spaced samples. It does not automatically prevent leakage from precomputed centered rolling features or vintage revisions; feature construction and preprocessing must be handled carefully inside the evaluation design.

Python example — genuine one-step rolling-origin evaluation:

import numpy as np
from sklearn.linear_model import Ridge
from sklearn.metrics import mean_absolute_error

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
y = 50 + np.linspace(0, 5, 80) + 4 * np.sin(np.arange(80) / 6)
y = y + rng.normal(scale=0.8, size=80)
predictions, actuals = [], []

for origin in range(35, len(y) - 1):
    # All historical targets are known through 'origin'.
    train_t = np.arange(12, origin + 1)
    X_train = np.column_stack([y[train_t - 1], y[train_t - 12]])
    model = Ridge(alpha=1.0).fit(X_train, y[train_t])
    x_next = np.array([[y[origin], y[origin - 11]]])
    predictions.append(model.predict(x_next)[0])
    actuals.append(y[origin + 1])

print('Rolling one-step MAE:', mean_absolute_error(actuals, predictions))

This evaluates an updating one-step forecast: once each new outcome arrives, it may be used at the next origin. Do not reuse the same code to claim you evaluated an h-step forecast issued all at once from a single origin.

NoteReference Answer

Specify an economically meaningful aggregation relationship, construct base forecasts at each level, and reconcile them so required geographic totals are coherent under chosen weights.

State-level price indices may not combine linearly into a national index using fixed unit weights: index definitions, sampling, geography, weighting schemes, and methodological revisions matter. Start by inspecting the actual published aggregation methodology.

Q21. Interview trap: Does a small Granger-causality p-value prove an economic intervention will change the outcome?

Difficulty: Hard · Added for interview prep

NoteReference Answer

No. It indicates lagged predictive content in a specified model, not an identified causal treatment effect. Common shocks, feedback, and omitted predictors can generate Granger relationships.

TipDetailed Explanation

Oil prices may Granger-predict inflation, but forecasting success does not identify the effect of an exogenous oil-price intervention. Inflation expectations, demand, policy responses, and supply shocks may jointly drive both series.

If asked whether a policy change causes a response, discuss structural identification using valid instruments, natural experiments, or defensible restrictions. Keep predictive and causal interpretations separate.

Q22. Interview trap: Should a model with smaller residual standard deviation always have tighter and better prediction intervals?

Difficulty: Hard · Added for interview prep

NoteReference Answer

No. Interval quality depends on innovation dynamics, the horizon, parameter and model uncertainty, non-Gaussian tails, covariate scenarios, and the calibration method.

TipDetailed Explanation

A highly flexible model can fit the training data almost perfectly and have tiny in-sample residuals, yet fail after a structural break. Its narrow Gaussian intervals might cover only 60% of future observations despite claiming 95% coverage.

Evaluate empirical interval coverage and width across rolling-origin backtests. Propagate parameter, covariate, and shock uncertainty as appropriate; do not equate a good in-sample fit with a useful probability forecast.

Q23. How would you explain a forecast failure to a nontechnical stakeholder?

Difficulty: Hard · Added for interview prep

NoteReference Answer

Separate the forecast target, known information at issuance, error size, uncertainty band, and plausible failure mechanisms. Explain which changes will be tested and what baseline protects decisions while the model is improved.

TipDetailed Explanation

A useful statement is: “We predicted quarterly HPA of +0.5% with a 90% interval from -1% to +2%; actual appreciation was -2%. We were outside the interval. We are investigating whether the sudden mortgage-rate shock, delayed employment data, or a model shift explains the miss.”
